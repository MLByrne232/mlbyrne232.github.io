Why GMA deformations?

#0000, 13:50, 27/11/26

Galois deformations are fun. If I give you a representation $\overline{\rho} : G \to \mathrm{GL}_n (\mathbb{F}_p)$ of the absolute Galois group $G=G_{\mathbb{Q}}$ of $\mathbb{Q}$, a deformation of $\overline{\rho}$ is just a lift $\rho_R : G_{\mathbb{Q}} \to \mathrm{GL}_n(R)$ through some quotient $R \to \mathbb{F}_p$ (or, to be precise, it is an equivalence class of lifts). Deformation theory then aims to classify all of the deformations $\rho_R$ of a given residual representation $\overline{\rho}$, though for technical reasons we usually only look at deformations valued over commutative, complete, Noetherian local rings with residue field $\mathbb{F}_p$ (we call these rings coefficient rings). The prototypical theorem of Galois deformation theory is as follows:

Theorem. If $\mathrm{End}_G (\overline\rho) = \mathbb{F}_p$, then there is a coefficient ring $\mathcal{R}^\text{u}$ and a deformation $\boldsymbol{\rho}^\text{u} : G \to \mathrm{GL}_n(\mathcal{R}^\text{u})$ such that every deformation $\rho : G \to \mathrm{GL}_n(R)$ is uniquely of the form $\rho = \varphi \circ \boldsymbol\rho^\text{u}$, where $\varphi : \mathcal{R}^\text{u} \to R$ is a continuous local homomorphism. We call $\boldsymbol\rho^\text{u}$ a universal deformation, and $\mathcal{R}^\text{u}$ a universal deformation ring.

As you may imagine, knowing that all deformations all come from one really big deformation is very useful, and so we may want to loosen the restriction on $\overline\rho$. It can be shown, however, that not every residual representation admits a (classical) deformation ring! To remedy this, we turn to generalised matrix algebras, or GMAs. Intuitively, a GMA $\mathcal{E}$ over a ring $R$ is something that behaves roughly like $\mathrm{GL}_n(R).$ The formal definition is in the literature (cf. [WWE19, Sec. 3.1]), but for now we will give a cursory definition of $2 \times 2$ GMAs over $R$.

Definition. Suppose we have two $R$-modules $B,C$ and a morphism $\mu : B \otimes_R C \to R$ which is 'associative'. The generalised matrix algebra $\mathcal{E}$ associated to the data $(R, B, C, \mu)$ is \[ \mathcal{E} := \begin{bmatrix} R & B \\ C & R \end{bmatrix}, \] whose elements are formal matrices \[ \begin{pmatrix}r_1 & b \\ c & r_2\end{pmatrix} \in \begin{bmatrix} R & B \\ C & R \end{bmatrix} \] with $r_i \in R, b \in B,$ and $c \in C,$ and whose multiplication is given by \[ \begin{pmatrix}r_1 & b \\ c & r_2\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}r_1' & b' \\ c' & r_2'\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}r_1 r_1' + \mu(b, c') & r_1 b' + r_2' b \\ r_1'c + r_2 c' & r_2 r_2' + \mu(b', c)\end{pmatrix}. \] We additionally define the trace and determinant functions of $\mathcal{E}$ by \[ \mathrm{tr}_{\mathcal{E}} \begin{pmatrix}r_1 & b \\ c & r_2\end{pmatrix} = r_1 + r_2, \qquad \mathrm{det}_{\mathcal{E}} \begin{pmatrix}r_1 & b \\ c & r_2\end{pmatrix} = r_1 r_2 - \mu(b, c). \]

A GMA representation is then a (continuous) homomorphism $\rho : G \to \mathcal{E}^\times$, and we say that such $\rho$ deforms $\overline{\rho}$ if the character $\mathrm{det}_{\mathcal{E}} \circ \rho$ is a (classical) deformation of the character $\det \circ \overline\rho$. We then find...

Theorem. If $\overline\rho$ is 'multiplicity-free' (i.e. all irreducible factors of $\overline\rho$ occur with multiplicity $\leq 1$), then there exists: a coefficient ring $\mathcal{R}^\text{GMA}$; a GMA $\mathcal{E}^\text{u}$ over $\mathcal{R}^\text{GMA}$; and a GMA deformation $\boldsymbol\rho^\text{GMA} : G \to (\mathcal{E}^\text{u})^\times$ of $\overline\rho$ such that every $GMA$ deformation $\rho : G \to E^\times$ of $\overline\rho$ factors through $\boldsymbol\rho^\text{GMA}$. In particular, if $\mathrm{End}_G(\overline\rho) = \mathbb{F}_p$ then $\boldsymbol\rho^\text{u}$ is such a GMA deformation.

So we have a more general class of residuals which admit universal deformation rings! There is an even more general class of things called 'pseudorepresentations', but at this point our 'pseudodeformations' are no longer necessarily homomorphisms! GMA deformation theory then sits as a perfect compromise: just general enough to capture all interesting examples, and still specific enough to retain a similar structure to that of the residual object.
